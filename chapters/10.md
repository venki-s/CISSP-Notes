# CISSP — Chapter 10: Physical Security Requirements

**Domains:** Security Architecture and Engineering (Domain 3); Security Operations (Domain 7)  
**Study focus:** Physical security controls, facility design, environmental hazards, power, and fire protection. Long operational explanations from the study guide are intentionally shortened.

## 1. Physical Security Fundamentals

- **Life safety comes first.** Protect people before equipment, data, or business continuity.
- **Physical access can bypass logical controls:** An intruder with direct access may steal, damage, or tamper with equipment.
- **Secure facility plan:** Based on risk assessment and **critical path analysis**—identifying the people, power, networks, cooling, equipment, and other dependencies that keep critical business processes running.
- **Technology convergence:** Combining services onto one infrastructure may save costs but can create a **single point of failure (SPOF)**.
- **Site selection:** Consider natural disasters, flooding, nearby hazardous facilities, access by emergency responders, building materials, entrances, and power availability.
- **Defense in depth:** Place multiple barriers in sequence, with stronger access restrictions around more sensitive assets.

### Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design (CPTED)

**CPTED = Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design:** Shape the physical environment to discourage crime and make suspicious activity more visible.

| First-generation CPTED concept | Easy meaning / example |
|---|---|
| **Access control** | Guide visitors through limited, visible entry points. |
| **Natural surveillance** | Clear sightlines, good lighting, visible entrances and walkways. |
| **Image and milieu** | Well-maintained premises convey oversight and care. |
| **Territorial control** | Signs, boundaries, landscaping, and ownership cues define protected spaces. |

**Second-generation CPTED:** Social cohesion, community culture, connectivity, and threshold capacity (the community's ability to absorb change without losing stability). **CPTED supplements—not replaces—locks, guards, fences, and alarms.**

## 2. Functions of Physical Security Controls

**Remember the six D's in order:**

1. **Deter** — Discourage intrusion (signs, lighting, visible guards).
2. **Deny** — Block entry (locked gates, access-controlled doors).
3. **Detect** — Notice intrusion (motion detectors, alarms, cameras).
4. **Delay** — Slow the intruder (reinforced doors, barriers, cable locks).
5. **Determine** — Assess what happened (guards, logs, video review).
6. **Decide** — Choose and execute the response (security intervention, escalation).

**Control types:** Administrative (policies, training), technical (badge readers, sensors), and physical (fences, doors, bollards, guards).

## 3. Perimeter and Access Controls

| Control | Purpose / CISSP clue |
|---|---|
| **Fence / gate** | Defines and restricts the perimeter; gates should be as secure as the fence. |
| **Bollards** | Stop or slow vehicles from ramming a building or entrance. |
| **Turnstile** | Controls the direction and number of people passing through an entrance. |
| **Person trap (mantrap)** | Two controlled doors; verifies entry and helps prevent **tailgating/piggybacking**. |
| **Lighting** | Primarily a **deterrent**; improves visibility for guards and cameras. |
| **Security guards** | Can exercise judgment, investigate, and respond; require training and oversight. |
| **Smartcard / badge** | Identifies or authenticates a person; card = **something you have**. Combine with a **Personal Identification Number (PIN)** or biometrics for **Multifactor Authentication (MFA)**. |
| **Visitor escort and logs** | Limits visitor access, establishes accountability and physical audit trails. |

**Fence-height cues from the guide:** 3–4 feet deters casual trespass; 6–7 feet deters many intruders; 8+ feet with barbed/razor wire is a stronger barrier. These are illustrative, not universal requirements.

**Important:** Emergency egress must protect people. Physical access controls must follow fire and life-safety rules.

## 4. Intrusion Detection, Cameras, and Alarms

| Detection method | What it notices |
|---|---|
| **Passive Infrared (PIR)** | Changes in body heat/infrared radiation. |
| **Microwave / ultrasonic** | Changes in reflected wave patterns due to movement. |
| **Photoelectric** | Interruption/change in light or a beam. |
| **Capacitance** | Changes in an electrical/magnetic field. |
| **Balanced Magnetic Switch (BMS)** | Door/window opening. |
| **Dual-technology sensor** | Uses two detection technologies to help reduce false alarms. |
| **Closed-Circuit Television (CCTV)** | Surveillance, deterrence, and evidence; recorded video is also useful for investigation. |

- **Intrusion Detection System (IDS):** Detects unauthorized physical access; an alarm needs a **response** to be effective.
- **Alarm power:** Battery backup protects against power cuts; a **heartbeat/line supervision** signal can detect cut or disrupted alarm communications.
- **Alarm effect:** **Deterrent** (makes progress difficult), **repellent** (siren/lights drive intruder away), or **notification** (alerts security/authorities, possibly silently).
- **Alarm delivery:** **Local** (on-site siren), **central station** (monitored off-site), **auxiliary** (notifies emergency services).
- **False positives:** Use secondary verification (e.g., sensor plus camera review). Visible cameras mainly **deter**; monitored/recorded cameras also help **detect/investigate**.

## 5. Secure Rooms, Storage, and Work Areas

- **Main Distribution Frame (MDF):** Primary building network/telecom distribution or provider connection point. **Intermediate Distribution Frame (IDF):** Floor/section wiring closet connecting local cabling to the backbone.
- **Wiring closets:** Restrict and log entry; secure patch panels and cables; avoid unrelated storage and flammable materials. A **Protective Distribution System (PDS)** protects cables against tampering or interception.
- **Data center / server room:** Prefer an **interior/core location** away from exterior exposure, top floors, basements, water, gas, and sewage lines where practical. Use restricted access, monitoring, suitable cooling, and fire protection.
- **Media storage:** Locked storage; check-in/check-out logs, custodian accountability, classification-based protection, and sanitization of reusable media.
- **Evidence storage:** Restricted access, encryption, integrity hashes, logging, and ideally segregation from production systems. Preserve evidence integrity and chain of custody.
- **Restricted work areas:** Clear classification zones, visitor escorts, full-height partitions where appropriate, and a **clean-desk policy** to prevent accidental disclosure.
- **Sensitive Compartmented Information Facility (SCIF):** Specially secured area for handling **Sensitive Compartmented Information (SCI)**, with strict entry and recording-device controls.

## 6. Power Protection and Reliability

| Protection | Role |
|---|---|
| **Surge protector** | Limits transient high-voltage damage; does not provide backup power. |
| **Power conditioner** | Filters power disturbances and electrical noise. |
| **Uninterruptible Power Supply (UPS)** | Short-term battery power and power conditioning, bridging an outage or allowing safe shutdown. |
| **Generator** | Longer-duration backup power after startup; **UPS bridges the startup gap**. |

**UPS comparison:**
- **Double-conversion / online UPS:** Continuously conditions/converts incoming power; provides highly stable output without a transfer interruption under normal operation.
- **Line-interactive UPS:** Normally uses utility power with voltage regulation; switches to battery when needed, with a possible brief transition.

**Power problem terms (as described by the guide):**

| Short / momentary | Longer / sustained |
|---|---|
| **Fault:** Complete power interruption | **Blackout:** Prolonged complete outage |
| **Sag:** Low voltage | **Brownout:** Prolonged low voltage |
| **Spike:** High voltage | **Surge:** Prolonged high voltage |

- **Inrush:** Brief high current when equipment starts or connects to power.
- **Mean Time to Failure (MTTF):** Expected operating time until failure, commonly for nonrepairable items.
- **Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF):** Average operating time between failures of a repairable system.
- **Mean Time to Repair (MTTR):** Average time needed to repair/restore an item.
- **Service-Level Agreement (SLA):** Contractual response and service commitments for repairs/support.

## 7. Environmental Protection

- **Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning (HVAC):** Maintains acceptable temperature, humidity, airflow, and air quality.
- **Hot aisle / cold aisle:** Arrange racks so cool intake air and hot exhaust air are separated, improving cooling efficiency.
- **High humidity → condensation/corrosion; low humidity → static buildup and Electrostatic Discharge (ESD).** Use monitoring and environmental controls; apply equipment-specific operating ranges.
- **Plenum:** Space used for airflow distribution. Cabling in these spaces may need **plenum-rated** materials that limit smoke/toxic emissions.
- **Positive air pressure / filtration:** Helps keep dust and contaminants out of sensitive rooms.
- **Water damage:** Keep critical systems away from pipes and flood-prone areas; use water-leak detection and drainage planning.
- **Electromagnetic Interference (EMI)** and **Radio-Frequency Interference (RFI):** Can disrupt equipment and communication; mitigate with grounding, shielding, cable routing, and fiber-optic connections where suitable.

## 8. Fire Prevention, Detection, and Suppression

**First priority: evacuate and protect people.** The **fire triangle** is **heat + fuel + oxygen**; sustained combustion also involves a chemical chain reaction (often shown as a fire *tetrahedron*). Removing an essential element stops the fire.

### Fire stages

1. **Incipient:** Earliest stage; combustion products/ionization may be present before visible smoke.
2. **Smoke:** Smoke becomes detectable/visible.
3. **Flame:** Visible flames.
4. **Heat:** Intense heat and widespread burning.

**Detection clues:** Aspirating/incipient detection is designed for very early warning; smoke detectors detect smoke particles; fixed-temperature detectors trigger at a threshold; rate-of-rise detectors trigger on rapid temperature increase; flame detectors sense flame radiation.

### Fire classes (U.S. classification used in the guide)

| Class | Burning material | Suitable approach / reminder |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Ordinary combustibles: wood, paper, cloth | Water or suitable Class A agents. |
| **B** | Flammable liquids | Appropriate foam, carbon dioxide, or dry chemical agents. |
| **C** | Energized electrical equipment | Nonconductive extinguishing agent; **do not use ordinary water while energized**. |
| **D** | Combustible metals | Specialized Class D dry powder; not ordinary extinguishers. |
| **K** | Cooking fats and oils | Appropriate wet chemical agent. |

### Water suppression systems

| System | CISSP distinction |
|---|---|
| **Wet pipe** | Water is already in pipes; quickest discharge on sprinkler activation. |
| **Dry pipe** | Pipes hold air/inert gas; water enters after activation, causing a delay. |
| **Preaction** | Detection first permits water into pipes, then sprinkler activation releases it; helps reduce accidental discharge onto electronics. |
| **Deluge** | Open discharge heads supplied after release-system activation; floods the protected area rapidly. |

**Exam clue:** **Preaction** is often preferred among *water-based* systems for computer areas because it reduces accidental water release.

### Gaseous suppression

- **Carbon Dioxide (CO₂):** Effective but can create lethal asphyxiation hazards; personnel safety is critical.
- **Halon:** Effective but ozone-depleting and heavily restricted; alternatives include clean-agent systems such as **FM-200** (a halon replacement mentioned in the guide).
- **Clean agents:** Can reduce equipment damage compared with water; evaluate design, occupancy, exposure limits, evacuation alarms, and applicable fire codes before selection.

## 9. Occupant Safety, Continuity, and Physical Security Metrics

- **Occupant Emergency Plan (OEP):** Evacuation, shelter, personnel safety, and immediate emergency response—not primarily IT restoration.
- **Business Continuity Plan (BCP):** Sustain essential business operations through disruptions.
- **Disaster Recovery Plan (DRP):** Restore affected systems, services, and infrastructure after a disaster.
- **Key Performance Indicators (KPIs):** Track intrusion attempts, false alarms, time to detect/respond, downtime, and recovery performance. Test controls and review incident lessons learned.

## 10. Rapid CISSP Review — Commonly Confused Concepts

| Scenario / keyword | Think of |
|---|---|
| Building layout designed to discourage crime | **Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design (CPTED)** |
| Dependencies needed to keep critical functions working | **Critical path analysis** |
| A control that slows an attacker until guards arrive | **Delay** |
| Two-door controlled entry that prevents unauthorized following | **Person trap / mantrap** |
| Loss of alarm communication must itself trigger an alert | **Heartbeat / line supervision** |
| Short-term power while generator starts | **Uninterruptible Power Supply (UPS)** |
| Long-duration outage | **Generator** |
| Instant low voltage vs. sustained low voltage | **Sag vs. brownout** |
| Unexpected static discharges in equipment room | **Low humidity / Electrostatic Discharge (ESD)** |
| Unauthorized visitor vs. physical access record | **Escort policy + access logs** |
| Most suitable water-based sprinkler design for sensitive electronics | **Preaction** |
| Suppressing energized electrical equipment fire | **Class C; nonconductive agent** |
| People must be protected before hardware | **Life safety** |
| Worker evacuation vs. restoring IT | **Occupant Emergency Plan (OEP) vs. Disaster Recovery Plan (DRP)** |
