### 1.3 Laws

European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).   
. Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) - 1986.     
  . Based off of Comprehensive Crime Control Act of 1984 (CCCA).  
  . Makes it a crime to (without authorization):      
  1. Access classified info or financial info in a federal system.     
  2. Access a computer exclusively used by the federal government.     
  3. Use a federal computer to perpetrate fraud.     
  4. Cause malicious damage to a federal computer system in excess of $1000.     
  5. Modify medical records on a computer when it may impair medical care.    
  6. Traffic in computer passwords if it affects interstate commerce or involves a federal computer system.   

When Congress passed the CFAA, it raised the threshold of damage from $1,000 to $5,000 but also dramatically altered the scope of the regulation.    

  1. Any computer used exclusively by the U.S. government.   
  2. Any computer used exclusively by a financial institution.   
  3. Any computer used by the government or a financial institution when the offense impedes the ability of  the government or institution to use that system.   
  4. Any combination of computers used to commit an offense when they are not all located in the same state.   

CFAA Amendments computer Abuse Amendments Act of 1994

  1. Outlawed the creation of any type of malicious code that might cause damage to a computer system.   
  2. Modified the CFAA to cover any computer used in interstate commerce rather than just “federal interest” computer systems.   
  3. Allowed for the imprisonment of offenders, regardless of whether they actually intended to cause damage.   
  4. Provided legal authority for the victims of computer crime to pursue civil action to gain injunctive relief and compensation for damages.   
  5. Since the initial CFAA amendments in 1994, Congress passed additional amendments in 1996, 2001, 2002, and 2008 as part of other cybercrime legislation. 


  The National Information Infrastructure Protection Act (NIIPA) included the following main new areas of coverage: (1996)

  1. Broadens the CFAA to cover computer systems used in international commerce in addition to systems used in interstate commerce.  
  2. Extends similar protections to portions of the national infrastructure other than computing systems, such as railroads, gas pipelines, electric power grids, and telecommunications circuits.  
  3. Treats any intentional or reckless act that causes damage to critical portions of the national infrastructure as a felony.  


The Federal Information Security Management Act (FISMA), passed in 2002

 1. Requires/mandates program to cover agency's operations
 2. Requires/Mandates that government agencies include the activities of contractors.  
 3. FISMA repealed/replaced two earlier laws: the Computer Security Act of 1987 and the Government Information Security Reform Act of 2000.
 4. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), responsible for developing the FISMA.   
 5. Periodic assessments of risk.   
 6. Policies and procedures that are based on risk assessments.   
 7. Subordinate plans for providing adequate information as appropriate.   
 8. Security awareness training to inform personnel.  
 9. Periodic testing and evaluation of the effectiveness of information security policies, procedures and process and practices.   
 10. A process addressing any deficiencies in the information security policies, procedures, and practices of the organization
 11. Procedures for detecting, reporting, and responding to security incidents.   
 12. Plans and procedures to ensure continuity of operations for information systems that support the operations and assets of the organization

Federal Cybersecurity Laws of 2014    

The first of these was the Federal Information Security Modernization Act of 2014, which amended the 2002 version of FISMA. The 2014 FISMA modified the rules of the 2002 FISMA by centralizing federal cybersecurity responsibility with the Department of Homeland Security (DHS).    
The following are commonly used NIST standards:   

  1. NIST SP 800-53: Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations. This standard is required for use in federal computing systems and is also commonly used as an industry cybersecurity benchmark.    
  2. NIST SP 800-171: Protecting Controlled Unclassified Information in Nonfederal Systems and Organizations. Compliance with this standard's security controls (which are quite similar to those found in NIST 800-53) is often included as a contractual requirement by government agencies. Federal contractors must often comply with NIST SP 800-171.   
  3. The NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) is a set of standards designed to serve as a voluntary risk-based framework for securing information and systems. 


### 1.4 Intellectual Property

1. Intangible assets are collectively referred to as intellectual property. 
2. Copyright and the Digital Millennium Copyright Act.   
3. Copyright law - Guarantees the creators of original works of authorship are protected from unauthorized duplication of their work. 
   Works covered:
   1. Literary.  
   2. Musical.  
   3. Dramatic.  
   4. Pantomimes and choreographic.  
   5. Pictorial, graphical, and sculptural.  
   6. Motion pictures and other audiovisual.  
   7. Sound recordings.   
   8. Architectural.    
4. Computer software is covered under the scope of literary work - the actual source code.    
5. Registering a copyright is not needed for copyright enforcement.   
6. Creators of work have an automatic copyright.   
7. Work for hire - when an employer pays for work in the normal course of a work day, the employer owns the copyright.   
8. Copyrights last until 70 years after the death of the last surviving author.  
9. Anonymous works are protected for 95 years from first publication or 120 years from date of creation - whichever is shorter.    

Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA), enacted in 1998.   

1. Rirst major provision of DMCA is prohibition of attempts to circumvent copyright protection mechanisms placed on a protected work by the copyright holder.    
2. The DMCA also limits the liability of Internet service providers (ISPs) when their circuits are used by criminals violating the copyright law.    
3. The DMCA provides for penalties of up to $1 million and 10 years in prison for repeat offenders.    


The acceptance of a trademark application in the United States depends on these two main requirements:    
1. The trademark must not be confusingly similar to another trademark.   
2. The trademark should not be descriptive of the goods and services that you will offer.   

Utility patents protect the intellectual property rights of inventors. They provide a period of 20 years from the time of the invention
1. The invention must be new.  
2. The invention must be useful.  
3. The invention must not be obvious.   

Trade Secrets/Stealing

1. Any individual found guilty of stealing trade secrets from a U.S. corporation with the intention of benefiting a foreign government or agent may be fined up to $500,000 and imprisoned for up to 15 years. Organizations found guilty may be fined up to $10,000,000.
2. Any individual found guilty of stealing trade secrets under other circumstances may be fined and imprisoned for up to 10 years. Organizations found guilty may be fined up to $5,000,000.

### 1.5 Software Licensing

Different softwar licenses

1. Perpetual Licenses This type of license allows users to pay a onetime fee for the software.   
2. Subscription Licenses Unlike perpetual licenses, subscription licenses are time-bound.    
3. Open-Source Licenses Open-source software is usually free to use, modify, and distribute. However, there are various open source licenses, each with its conditions, like the GNU General Public License (GPL) or the MIT License.  
4. Freeware Software that is available free of charge. It might come with restrictions on usage or lack features available in a paid version.    
5. Enterprise License Agreements (ELAs) These are comprehensive agreements between software vendors and large organizations.    
6. End-User License Agreements (EULAs) EULAs define the rights and restrictions that apply when using the software.   
7. Concurrent Use Licenses This allows a set number of users to access the software at the same time. Once the limit is reached, additional users must wait until a slot becomes available.
8. Named User Licenses This type of license is tied to specific users.   
8. Cloud Services License Agreements These agreements pertain to software-as-a-service (SaaS) provided over the Internet.
