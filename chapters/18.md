## 1. Business Continuity vs. Disaster Recovery

### Business Continuity Planning (BCP)
- Business-focused planning to maintain critical operations during disruptions.
- Identifies critical business processes and their recovery priorities.

### Disaster Recovery Planning (DRP)
- Technical complement to BCP.
- Focuses on recovering IT systems, infrastructure, and services after disruption.
- Goal: Restore critical operations within defined recovery objectives.

### Business Continuity Management (BCM)
- Umbrella discipline encompassing BCP, DRP, and crisis management.

### Important
- BCP determines what business functions must continue.
- DRP determines how supporting systems and services are recovered.
- A disaster is an event that interrupts or threatens critical business operations.
- DRP should contain predetermined procedures to reduce decision-making during emergencies.

## 2. Disaster Types and Risk Planning

### Natural Disasters
- Earthquakes, floods, storms, fires, pandemics, and other natural events.

### Human-Made Disasters
- Terrorism, explosions, power outages, infrastructure failures, hardware/software failures, strikes, theft, and vandalism.

### Planning Principles
- Assess threats relevant to each geographic location.
- Prioritize protection according to likelihood and business impact.
- Consider dependencies on personnel, utilities, suppliers, and transportation.
- Avoid concentrating recovery sites within the same disaster risk area.
- Ensure alternate facilities do not share critical single points of failure.

### Power Protection
- **UPS:** Short-term power during outages; supports shutdown or generator startup.
- **Generator:** Provides longer-term power, dependent on fuel availability.

**Exam:** Personnel safety takes priority over equipment and data recovery.

---

## 3. System Resilience, Fault Tolerance, and High Availability

### Single Point of Failure (SPOF)
- A component whose failure can cause the entire system or service to fail.

### Resilience
- Ability to withstand adverse events and maintain or regain acceptable service.

### Fault Tolerance
- Ability to continue operating despite component failure.
- Achieved through redundancy.

### High Availability (HA)
- Uses redundant components, failover, and load balancing to minimize downtime.
- May involve brief interruptions during failover.

### Failover vs. Failback
- **Failover:** Transfer processing to an alternate system after failure.
- **Failback:** Return processing to the original system after recovery.

### Load Balancing
- Distributes workload among multiple servers.
- Improves scalability and availability.
- Can remove failed servers from active processing.

**Exam:** Eliminate SPOFs to improve availability and resilience.

## 4. RAID and Disk Fault Tolerance

| RAID | Technique | Minimum Disks | Failure Tolerance |
|---|---|---|---|
| RAID 0 | Striping | 2 | None |
| RAID 1 | Mirroring | 2 | 1 disk |
| RAID 5 | Striping + distributed parity | 3 | 1 disk |
| RAID 6 | Striping + dual parity | 4 | 2 disks |
| RAID 10 | Stripe of mirrors | 4 | Multiple, depending on which disks fail |

### Important
- **RAID 0:** Performance improvement; no redundancy.
- **RAID 1:** Duplicate data on mirrored drives.
- **RAID 5:** Single-disk fault tolerance using parity.
- **RAID 6:** Dual-disk fault tolerance.
- **RAID 10:** Combines mirroring and striping; failure of both disks in one mirror pair causes array failure.

### Additional Terms
- **Hot Swapping:** Replace components without shutting down the system.
- **Cold Swapping:** System must be shut down before replacement.
- **Hot Spare:** Standby disk used to replace a failed array disk.

**Critical:** RAID provides fault tolerance, NOT backup protection. It does not protect against all corruption, deletion, or catastrophic failures.

## 5. Trusted Recovery

### Definition
- Ensures a system returns to a secure state following failure or crash.
- Security controls, configurations, and integrity must remain effective after recovery.

### Fail-Secure vs. Fail-Open
- **Fail-Secure:** Denies access when a failure occurs.
- **Fail-Open:** Allows access when a failure occurs.
- Selection depends on security, availability, and safety requirements.

### Common Criteria — Four Recovery Types

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| Manual Recovery | Administrator must restore a secure state |
| Automated Recovery | System automatically recovers from at least one type of failure |
| Automated Recovery Without Undue Loss | Automatic recovery while protecting designated objects from loss |
| Function Recovery | Automatically restores specific functions or securely rolls back failed recovery |

### Trusted Recovery Requirements
- Restore files and services.
- Verify security settings and classifications.
- Prevent unauthorized access during recovery.
- Ensure the recovered system is at least as secure as before the failure.

## 6. Quality of Service (QoS)

- Protects network service availability and performance during load.
- Prioritizes traffic according to business requirements.

### Key Parameters
- **Bandwidth:** Network transmission capacity.
- **Latency:** Time for a packet to travel between endpoints.
- **Jitter:** Variation in packet latency.
- **Packet Loss:** Packets lost during transmission.
- **Interference:** Disturbances that degrade communications.

**Exam:** Prioritizing time-sensitive traffic such as voice/video → QoS.

## 7. Business Impact Analysis and Recovery Objectives

- BIA identifies and prioritizes critical business functions.
- DR recovery priorities must align with BIA findings.
- Consider financial, operational, legal, personnel, and contractual impact.

### Key Recovery Metrics

| Metric | Meaning |
|---|---|
| MTD | Maximum Tolerable Downtime — longest acceptable business interruption |
| RTO | Recovery Time Objective — target time to restore operations |
| RPO | Recovery Point Objective — maximum acceptable data loss measured in time |
| MTTR | Mean Time to Repair — average time required to repair a failed component/system |

### Important Distinctions
- **RTO:** How quickly must the system recover?
- **RPO:** How much recent data can be lost?
- **MTD:** How long can the business tolerate disruption?
- **MTTR:** How long does repair typically take?

### Planning
- RTO should fit within the business's maximum tolerable downtime.
- Lower RTO and RPO generally require more expensive recovery capabilities.
- Recover critical processes according to BIA priorities, not simply department importance.

## 8. Alternate Recovery Sites

| Site | Infrastructure | Data | Cost | Recovery Speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cold | Facility and basic utilities | Not available | Lowest | Slowest |
| Warm | Equipment and communications ready | Usually requires restoration | Medium | Moderate |
| Hot | Fully configured operational environment | Replicated/recent data | Highest | Fastest |

### Cold Site
- No preinstalled computing equipment or active broadband services.
- Requires equipment installation, configuration, and data restoration.
- Recovery may take weeks.

### Warm Site
- Preconfigured equipment and communications.
- Data generally restored from backups.
- Faster than cold, slower than hot.

### Hot Site
- Fully equipped and ready to assume operations.
- Uses periodic or continuous data replication.
- Can support recovery within seconds or minutes, depending on design.

### Other Options
- **Mobile Site:** Transportable recovery facility.
- **Cloud Recovery:** On-demand infrastructure and stored system images.
- **Multiple Processing Sites:** Geographic diversity increases resilience.

### Important Risks
- Recovery sites must be geographically separated from common disasters.
- Shared recovery facilities may experience capacity shortages during widespread disasters.
- Hot sites holding production data require equivalent security controls.
- Cloud recovery may require a **resource capacity agreement** to guarantee needed resources.

---

## 9. Mutual Assistance Agreements (MAA)

- Also called **Reciprocal Agreements**.
- Two organizations agree to share computing resources following disasters.
- Can reduce recovery infrastructure costs.

### Risks
- Agreement may be difficult to enforce.
- Both organizations may be affected by the same disaster.
- Resource availability may not be guaranteed.
- Confidentiality and compliance concerns may arise.

**Exam:** Low-cost recovery through reciprocal resource sharing → MAA.

## 10. Database Recovery Strategies

| Technique | Data Transfer | Recovery Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Electronic Vaulting | Bulk database backup transfers | Potentially greater data loss; restoration required |
| Remote Journaling | Frequent transaction-log transfers | Logs applied during recovery |
| Remote Mirroring | Replicates changes to live remote database | Rapid failover; highest cost |

### Electronic Vaulting
- Transfers complete database backup copies to a remote location.
- Recovery depends on the last successfully transferred backup.

### Remote Journaling
- Transfers database transaction logs more frequently.
- Logs are stored remotely and applied during recovery.
- Remote database is not continuously maintained as a live replica.

### Remote Mirroring
- Maintains a live database copy at the alternate site.
- Replicates updates as they occur.
- Supports rapid recovery but requires more resources.

**Exam:** Electronic Vaulting → Remote Journaling → Remote Mirroring generally increases recovery speed and cost.

## 11. Backup Types

| Backup Type | Data Backed Up | Archive Bit After Backup | Restore Requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full | All selected data | Reset | Latest full |
| Incremental | Changes since last full/incremental | Reset | Full + all subsequent incrementals |
| Differential | Changes since last full | Unchanged | Full + latest differential |

### Key Points
- **Full:** Complete backup regardless of archive bit.
- **Incremental:** Typically smallest and fastest backup; slower restoration.
- **Differential:** Grows until next full backup; faster restoration than incremental.

### Archive Bit
- **1:** File modified since the applicable backup.
- **0:** Archive bit cleared after applicable backup.
- Some systems use timestamps instead of archive bits.

### Restoration
- Incremental: Restore full backup, followed by every incremental backup in order.
- Differential: Restore full backup, followed by only the latest differential backup.

**Exam:** Faster daily backup → Incremental. Fewer restore sets → Differential.

## 12. Backup Storage and Protection

### Storage Strategies
- Maintain backup copies at geographically separate locations.
- On-site backups support rapid local restoration.
- Off-site backups protect against site-wide disasters.
- Cloud storage provides scalable remote backup storage.

### Backup Technologies
- **D2D:** Disk-to-disk backup.
- **VTL:** Virtual Tape Library; disk storage emulates tape.
- **Tape Rotation:** Strategies include Grandfather-Father-Son (GFS) and Tower of Hanoi.

### Important Controls
- Align backup frequency with RPO.
- Align restore capability with RTO.
- Protect backup confidentiality and integrity.
- Account for storage growth and network bandwidth.
- Consider data residency and regulatory requirements.
- **Perform actual restore tests.** Successful backup logs alone do not prove recoverability.

## 13. Software Escrow

- Protects against failure or disappearance of a critical software supplier.
- Developer deposits current source code with an independent third party.
- Source code is released when specified contractual trigger events occur.

### Common Triggers
- Vendor bankruptcy.
- Vendor liquidation.
- Failure to meet support obligations.

**Exam:** Ensure continued maintenance of critical proprietary software when the vendor fails → Software Escrow.

## 14. Disaster Recovery Plan Implementation

### Disaster Declaration
- Define criteria for declaring a disaster.
- Specify who has authority to activate the DRP.

### Emergency Response
- Protect personnel first.
- Use prioritized response checklists.
- Initiate necessary notifications and recovery activities.

### Personnel and Communications
- Identify DR team roles and responsibilities.
- Maintain backup contacts and alternate communication methods.
- Use designated personnel for public/media communication.

### Assessment
- Evaluate damage, system availability, and recovery priorities.
- Reassess the situation as recovery progresses.

### DR Documentation
- Executive summaries.
- Department-specific plans.
- Technical recovery procedures.
- Recovery checklists.

### Security
- Restrict DRP access on a need-to-know basis.
- Maintain current copies at primary and alternate locations.
- Do not rely solely on systems that may be unavailable during a disaster.

---

## 15. Recovery vs. Restoration

### Recovery
- Restores critical business processes at an alternate location.
- Must meet defined recovery time requirements.
- Handled by the disaster recovery team.

### Restoration
- Repairs or rebuilds the original facility and infrastructure.
- May be handled by a separate salvage team.
- Includes moving operations back to the primary site.

### Failback
- Verify the restored facility before transferring critical processes.
- Return lower-priority services first when appropriate.
- Transfer business-critical services after validating stability.
- Emergency conditions end when normal operations are safely restored.

## 16. Disaster Recovery Testing

| Test | Description | Operational Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Read-Through | Personnel review DRP documents | Minimal |
| Tabletop | Team discusses a simulated disaster | Minimal |
| Walk-Through | Personnel rehearse procedures, potentially including physical actions | Low |
| Simulation | Selected recovery actions are tested without full production interruption | Moderate |
| Parallel | Alternate site operates while primary remains operational | Higher |
| Full Interruption | Primary operations shut down; recovery site takes over | Highest |

### Read-Through
- Verifies personnel understand assigned responsibilities.
- Identifies outdated information and missing role assignments.

### Tabletop
- Discussion-based role-playing of a disaster scenario.
- No actual recovery-site activation required.

### Walk-Through
- Rehearsal of procedures.
- May include physical movement or limited actions.

### Simulation
- Tests selected response measures in a controlled scenario.
- May affect noncritical operations.

### Parallel
- Recovery site is activated.
- Primary site continues normal operations.
- Validates alternate processing without stopping production.

### Full Interruption
- Primary operations are stopped.
- Workload transfers to recovery site.
- Most realistic but operationally risky.

**Exam:** Alternate site activated while primary remains live → Parallel Test.

## 17. DRP Training, Maintenance, and Lessons Learned

### Training
- Initial training for new DR personnel.
- Periodic refresher training.
- General awareness for employees.
- Ensure responsibilities are understood before disasters occur.

### Maintenance
- DRP is a living document.
- Update after infrastructure, personnel, business, or regulatory changes.
- Apply change management controls to plan updates.

### Lessons Learned
- Evaluate effectiveness of recovery actions.
- Identify deficiencies, delays, and procedural gaps.
- Document corrective actions and update the DRP.

### Test Communications
- Notify stakeholders before testing.
- Provide updates during disruptive tests.
- Conduct post-test debriefing.
- Maintain required records and notify regulators when applicable.

---

## Final CISSP Principles

- Business requirements and BIA drive recovery priorities.
- Personnel safety comes before equipment recovery.
- Recovery strategies must satisfy RTO and RPO.
- Geographic diversity reduces correlated disaster risk.
- Redundancy and RAID do not replace backups.
- Hot sites provide faster recovery at higher cost.
- Differential backups require fewer restore sets than incremental backups.
- DR plans require authorization, documentation, training, and testing.
- The highest-risk DR test is full interruption.
- Disaster recovery is incomplete until normal operations are restored.
